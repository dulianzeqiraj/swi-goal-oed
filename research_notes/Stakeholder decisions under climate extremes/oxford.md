## P1 — Kelder et al. (2020), UNSEEN trends: nonstationary 100-year rainfall from pooled seasonal hindcasts (Theme 2, intense rainfall)

### Takeaway
UNSEEN pools the ensemble members and lead times of a seasonal forecast system, which enlarges a 35-year record about 100-fold. A GEV whose location and scale drift linearly in time is then fitted to the pooled data, so changes in the 100-year event can be detected. For our model this gives a defensible way to set extreme recharge and surge inputs, and the shift radius δ̄, beyond what the observed record has shown.

### Cited Findings
- **Citation:** Kelder, T., Müller, M., Slater, L. J., Marjoribanks, T. I., Wilby, R. L., Prudhomme, C., Bohlinger, P., Ferranti, L. & Nipen, T. (2020). Using UNSEEN trends to detect decadal changes in 100-year precipitation extremes. *npj Climate and Atmospheric Science*, 3, article 47. DOI 10.1038/s41612-020-00149-4. Published November 2020. — [ADS record](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2020npCAS...3...47K/abstract); [Loughborough repository PDF s41612-020-00149-4.pdf](https://repository.lboro.ac.uk/articles/journal_contribution/Using_UNSEEN_trends_to_detect_decadal_changes_in_100-year_precipitation_extremes/13139330/1); [NORA open-access copy](https://nora.nerc.ac.uk/id/eprint/529184/)
- **Oxford author:** Louise J. Slater. The Oxford University Research Archive (ORA) holds the paper and lists Slater with a University of Oxford affiliation. The exact printed wording (e.g., "School of Geography and the Environment") was **not seen** — [ORA record](https://ora.ox.ac.uk/objects/uuid:e46b4c94-1db5-4fff-8a33-efde4750b489); [Slater's post announcing the paper, Oct 2020](https://louisejslater.com/2020/10/12/new-paper-unseen-trends/)
- **Method:** members and lead times from ECMWF SEAS5 are pooled to enlarge the historical record 100-fold. A GEV with a time covariate is fitted to track the 100-year precipitation value over 1981–2015. The case study is 3-day autumn precipitation extremes over Western Norway and Svalbard — [ADS abstract](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2020npCAS...3...47K/abstract); [search summary of the ORA/Loughborough copies](https://repository.lboro.ac.uk/articles/journal_contribution/Using_UNSEEN_trends_to_detect_decadal_changes_in_100-year_precipitation_extremes/13139330)
- **Ensemble structure:** 25 members, monthly initialisations, 7-month forecasts. Four initialisation months (May–August) forecast the target autumn at lead times of 2–5 months. The pooled set is described as a 3,500-year UNSEEN dataset (25 × 4 × 35 = 3,500) — [Kelder et al. 2022 workflow paper, Meteorological Applications](https://rmets.onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/10.1002/met.2065); [UNSEEN code repository](https://github.com/timokelder/UNSEEN)
- **Admissibility tests:** before pooling, three tests must pass: (i) ensemble-member independence, (ii) model stability (the extreme distribution must be similar across lead times) and (iii) fidelity against observations. The R package implements all three — [UNSEEN GitHub](https://github.com/timokelder/UNSEEN); [UNSEEN-open documentation](https://timokelder.github.io/UNSEEN-open/)
- **Nonstationary GEV law:** location and scale vary linearly with time. In standard notation, F(z; t) = exp{−[1 + ξ (z − μ(t))/σ(t)]^(−1/ξ)}, with μ(t) = μ₀ + μ₁t and σ(t) = σ₀ + σ₁t. The time-dependent T-year return level z_T(t) solves F(z_T(t); t) = 1 − 1/T. The linear location+scale form is reported in the search summary; the exact parametrisation in the paper (e.g., log-scale) was **not seen** — [EGU2020 presentation by the same authors](https://presentations.copernicus.org/EGU2020/EGU2020-521_presentation.pdf); [search summary](https://www.researchgate.net/publication/346522040_Using_UNSEEN_trends_to_detect_decadal_changes_in_100-year_precipitation_extremes)
- **Main quantitative results:** for Western Norway, uncertainty is substantially reduced relative to the observed record and there is no significant linear trend. For Svalbard there is a significant rise: a 100-year event estimated for 1981 has a return period of about 40 years by 2015 — [ADS abstract](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2020npCAS...3...47K/abstract); [EGU2020 abstract](https://meetingorganizer.copernicus.org/EGU2020/EGU2020-521.html)
- **Related Oxford-co-authored follow-ups (not covered earlier; not checked in detail):** Kelder et al. 2022, *Meteorological Applications* 29, e2065, DOI 10.1002/met.2065 (an open workflow for low-likelihood high-impact events), and Kelder et al. 2022, *Environmental Research Letters* ("Interpreting extreme climate impacts from large ensemble simulations — are they unseen or unrealistic?"; volume and article number not verified) — [Met Apps](https://rmets.onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/10.1002/met.2065); [ResearchGate listing of the ERL paper](https://www.researchgate.net/publication/359189022_Interpreting_extreme_climate_impacts_from_large_ensemble_simulations_-_Are_they_unseen_or_unrealistic)

### Inferences
- **Inputs it modifies:** (a) the recharge factor f_R driving q_in. Today f_R comes from a stationary AR(1) rainfall model (φ = 0.7) fitted to the short record. UNSEEN would replace its tails with an ensemble-based nonstationary law, f_R ~ g(z_T(t)), for both the wet tail (recharge pulses) and the dry tail (multi-season deficits; the GEV would be fitted to block minima of the 2–3-season accumulated anomaly). (b) The sea-boundary head h_s: the same pooling plus nonstationary GEV can be applied to Adriatic storm-surge maxima when a large hindcast ensemble exists.
- **Direction of the decision:** for seawater intrusion the dangerous tail is the dry one. If the UNSEEN dry-tail return level shifts upward the way the Svalbard wet tail did, the design drought used for well placement gets worse. The screen must then sit further inland or shallower, or b must be cut in the scenario, so pumping becomes more conservative. If the trend is not significant (the Norway case), UNSEEN still narrows the confidence band on z_T. The margin can then be reduced *without* loss of safety, which allows less conservative pumping.
- **SPIRANCA-GO link:** δ̄ (the strength of unrecorded shifts in the anchor span) is currently chosen by judgement. UNSEEN supplies physically simulated but unrecorded anchor values. One candidate rule: δ̄ = (ensemble 1-in-T anomaly of r)² / Var_obs(r), i.e., the squared ratio of the plausible-but-unseen rainfall shift to the recorded spread.
- **Innovation idea — "UNSEEN-certified δ̄" test:** treat each ensemble member as a pseudo-environment e. Fit the anchor-regression alarm on the observed record only. Then check empirically whether its certified coverage 1 − α at the 250 mg/L threshold holds on held-out members, i.e., whether P_e(Cl > 250 and no alarm) ≤ α for all e. The δ̄ to use is the smallest one that passes on ≥ 95% of members. Run Kelder's three admissibility tests on the rainfall input before pooling. Numerically, use an AR(1) rainfall model with drifting GEV innovations as a synthetic "SEAS5" ensemble and MONTIMI-GO for the chloride response.

### Gaps
- The printed affiliation lines of the npj article were not opened (nature.com blocked). The Oxford link rests on the ORA listing and Slater's Oxford position.
- No published UNSEEN application to Albanian or Adriatic rainfall, or to dry-tail recharge, was found. That application would be ours.
- The fidelity statistics and the exact GEV parametrisation were not read from the full text.

## P2 — Kew et al. (2021), attribution of drought trends in eastern Africa: precipitation versus warming (Themes 3 drought + 4 causal attribution)

### Takeaway
The paper uses counterfactual-style trend attribution against global warming for six eastern African regions over 1900–2018. Standardized soil moisture shows no discernible trend and precipitation trends are highly uncertain, while local temperature rises significantly. The authors judge that warming has so far had a limited effect on agricultural drought, and they recommend pairing soil-moisture analysis with a precipitation-deficit analysis. For us, this is a template for testing whether a warming-driven recharge shift lies outside the span of our rainfall anchor.

### Cited Findings
- **Citation:** Kew, S. F., Philip, S. Y., Hauser, M., Hobbins, M., Wanders, N., van Oldenborgh, G. J., van der Wiel, K., Veldkamp, T. I. E., Kimutai, J., Funk, C. & Otto, F. E. L. (2021). Impact of precipitation and increasing temperatures on drought trends in eastern Africa. *Earth System Dynamics*, 12(1), 17–35. DOI 10.5194/esd-12-17-2021 (CC BY 4.0) — [ESD article page](https://esd.copernicus.org/articles/12/17/2021/); [NOAA repository copy](https://repository.library.noaa.gov/view/noaa/30999)
- **Author list:** the search summary notes that some listings omit Chris Funk or order the authors differently. The ESD page is the reference — [ResearchGate listing](https://www.researchgate.net/publication/348278707_Impact_of_precipitation_and_increasing_temperatures_on_drought_trends_in_eastern_Africa)
- **Oxford author:** Friederike E. L. Otto, listed at the "School of Geography and the Environment, University of Oxford" according to search results drawn from the ESD article. The printed byline was not opened directly — [ESD article page](https://esd.copernicus.org/articles/12/17/2021/)
- **Method and scope:** trends from 1900 (as an approximation to pre-industrial conditions) to 2018 were studied for six eastern African regions. A combination of models and observational datasets was used for soil moisture, precipitation, temperature and evaporative demand — [ESD abstract](https://esd.copernicus.org/articles/12/17/2021/)
- **Main results:** no discernible trend in standardized soil moisture. Precipitation is the main driver of soil-moisture variability, especially in drier regions. Precipitation trend estimates carry large error margins with no clear direction. Local temperatures show significant positive trends. The impact of rising local temperatures on agricultural drought is limited so far. The paper recommends combining soil-moisture analyses with precipitation-deficit analyses — [ESD abstract](https://esd.copernicus.org/articles/12/17/2021/); [ESD preprint discussion files](https://esd.copernicus.org/preprints/esd-2019-20/esd-2019-20-AC1.pdf)
- **Statistical engine (context):** the World Weather Attribution protocol (Philip et al. 2020; already covered, so cited here only for context). Its standard trend model shifts the fitted distribution with smoothed global mean surface temperature T′: μ(T′) = μ₀ + αT′ for temperature-like variables, or μ = μ₀·exp(αT′/μ₀) with σ/μ fixed for precipitation-like variables. The probability ratio is PR = p₁/p₀ for an event threshold under present (T′₁) versus counterfactual (T′₀) climate. That Kew et al. used exactly this parametrisation was **not verified** from the text — [ESD article page](https://esd.copernicus.org/articles/12/17/2021/)

### Inferences
- **Input it modifies:** our drought driver. Recharge is effectively R = P − ET(T, …). SPIRANCA-GO assumes the "warming" shift lies in the span of the anchors (rainfall anomaly r, pumping anomaly). Kew et al. show how to separate a precipitation-driven trend from a temperature-driven one with a GMST covariate. If, in the Fushë-Kuqe catchment, the temperature/evaporative-demand part of the recharge trend is significant once r is held fixed, the warming shift has a component *orthogonal* to the anchor span. The certificate m(b) is then not valid as stated.
- **Direction of the decision:** if the orthogonal component is significant, add a temperature (or PET) anchor, or inflate the margin by a bound on the orthogonal shift. Either way the pumping becomes more conservative, and a PET record would need to be added to monitoring. If it is not significant, as in the eastern-Africa result, the current two-anchor design is supported. The Mediterranean climate may behave differently, so this must be tested locally rather than assumed.
- **Scenario change:** report the drought scenario for well placement as a counterfactual pair (1900-like versus present climate, via T′). The probability ratio PR_sal = P(J > 250 | T′₁)/P(J > 250 | T′₀) then becomes a stakeholder-facing attribution number for salinisation risk.
- **Innovation idea — "span test for warming":** regress the recharge proxy (or the chloride residual after the anchor fit) on smoothed T′ while controlling for r. Then test H₀: the T′ coefficient = 0. Under H₀ the SPIRANCA-GO margin stands. Under rejection, replace δ̄·max(0, ℓ(b)) by δ̄·max(0, ℓ(b)) + κ·|Δ_⊥|, where Δ_⊥ is the estimated orthogonal shift and κ the Lipschitz constant of J in recharge (obtainable from the MONTIMI-GO adjoint). Numerically: simulate R = P − ET(T) with rising T, generate chloride with MONTIMI-GO, and measure the alarm's miss rate with and without the correction.

### Gaps
- The quantitative probability ratios and trend magnitudes per region were not retrieved; only the qualitative abstract results are confirmed.
- Otto's printed affiliation was confirmed only through search-result text drawn from the ESD page, not by opening the PDF.

## P3 — Murgatroyd & Hall (2020), resilience of inter-basin transfers to spatially coherent severe droughts (Themes 3 drought + 1 decision/adaptive planning)

### Takeaway
The paper links climate, hydrology and water-resource models, using an extended historical flow record and a large climate ensemble. It shows that transfers between neighbouring water companies are vulnerable to droughts that hit both basins at the same time. Strategies with extra storage reduce this joint drought coincidence, while strategies without supporting infrastructure do less to build resilience. For us, the lesson is that any backup source or second wellfield must be judged on *joint* failure with the main well, not on its own.

### Cited Findings
- **Citation:** Murgatroyd, A. & Hall, J. W. (2020). The resilience of inter-basin transfers to severe droughts with changing spatial characteristics. *Frontiers in Environmental Science*, 8, article 571647. DOI 10.3389/fenvs.2020.571647. Received 11 June 2020, accepted 20 November 2020, published 8 December 2020 — [Frontiers full text](https://www.frontiersin.org/journals/environmental-science/articles/10.3389/fenvs.2020.571647/full)
- **Oxford authors:** both authors are at the Environmental Change Institute, University of Oxford, according to the Frontiers article page (as returned in search results) — [Frontiers full text](https://www.frontiersin.org/journals/environmental-science/articles/10.3389/fenvs.2020.571647/full); [OPSIS (Oxford) publication list](https://opsis.eci.ox.ac.uk/publication/)
- **Problem statement:** transfers are vulnerable to large-scale, spatially coherent droughts that may cause shortages in neighbouring basins at the same time. Under climate change, more severe droughts are also expected to cover larger areas — [Frontiers abstract](https://www.frontiersin.org/journals/environmental-science/articles/10.3389/fenvs.2020.571647/full)
- **Method:** integrated climate, hydrological and water-resource modelling of new transfer schemes between two neighbouring water companies in Southern England. An extended historical record of river flows and a large ensemble of climate-driven future flows are used to explore spatial and temporal variability — [Frontiers abstract](https://www.frontiersin.org/journals/environmental-science/articles/10.3389/fenvs.2020.571647/full). A related 2018 EGU abstract by the same group describes the large ensemble as HadCM3/HadRM3 runs through weather@home. That this exact ensemble was used in the 2020 paper is **unverified** — [EGU2018-15858](https://meetingorganizer.copernicus.org/EGU2018/EGU2018-15858.pdf)
- **Main results:** meteorological, hydrological and water-resource droughts all increase in frequency and intensity through the 21st century. A new transfer can increase supply resilience. Combined drought coincidence decreases in strategies with additional storage, while strategies without supporting infrastructure are less successful. The paper concludes that new infrastructure should be tested against droughts more extreme and spatially different from the historical record (scenario-based adaptive planning) — [Frontiers abstract](https://www.frontiersin.org/journals/environmental-science/articles/10.3389/fenvs.2020.571647/full); [DMDU Society summary](https://www.deepuncertainty.org/2021/01/18/the-resilience-of-inter-basin-transfers-to-severe-droughts-with-changing-spatial-characteristics-2020/)
- **The decision quantity in words:** the probability that both systems are in (water-resource) drought at once, compared across supply strategies. A natural formalisation, which is ours and not the paper's printed formula, is C = P(D_A ∩ D_B) and its excess over independence, C − P(D_A)P(D_B) — [Frontiers abstract](https://www.frontiersin.org/journals/environmental-science/articles/10.3389/fenvs.2020.571647/full)

### Inferences
- **What it modifies:** our drought scenario and objective. In a regional drought (AR(1), φ = 0.7), four things are positively associated: lower recharge f_R (less inland inflow q_in), higher private abstraction u, utility pumping b pushed up by demand, and any alternative source (a second wellfield, river intake or tanker supply) also in deficit. Modelling the supply well alone, or treating the backup as independent, makes the plan anti-conservative.
- **Direction of the decision:** evaluate placements and pumping rules on the joint event {J(x,b) > 250} ∩ {backup short}. Pumping becomes more conservative during multi-year droughts. Storage-type options (managed aquifer recharge before the dry season, or a protected reserve well kept idle in normal years) gain value relative to "more transfer capacity", mirroring the paper's storage result.
- **Innovation idea — "coincidence-aware placement" with an association inequality:** let D_A = {J(x,b) > 250} and D_B = {backup deficit}. Both are increasing functions of the same deficit sequence (−r_t). By Harris–FKG, for monotone functions of associated variables, P(D_A ∩ D_B) ≥ P(D_A)P(D_B). The product rule therefore *always* underestimates joint failure. Proposed rule: choose x to minimise P(D_A ∩ D_B) subject to the 250 mg/L chance constraint, and report the coincidence excess as a stakeholder number. Numerically: drive MONTIMI-GO and a simple backup-storage model with the same AR(1) rainfall, and compare the placement chosen under independence with the joint-risk placement.

### Gaps
- Numerical results (coincidence probabilities, failure frequencies per strategy) were not retrieved, nor the names of the two companies or schemes.
- The exact climate ensemble and the drought indicators used could not be confirmed from the full text.

## P4 — Becher et al. (2023), a multi-hazard stress test of a national water-supply system using "customer disruption days" (Themes 1 decision/risk metric + 2 floods/cyclones + 3 drought)

### Takeaway
The paper puts drought, flood and cyclone damage on one decision scale, customer disruption days (CDD), and propagates present and end-of-century climate scenarios through Jamaica's national water-supply network. Frequent droughts dominate expected disruption, while rare asset-damage extremes cause the most widespread outages. End-of-century climate raises disruption by about 2 to 2.5 times. For us, this is a template for replacing the binary "chloride > 250" goal with a risk-integrated disruption metric that covers both slow salinisation and fast storm-surge or flood events.

### Cited Findings
- **Citation:** Becher, O., Pant, R., Verschuur, J., Mandal, A., Paltan, H., Lawless, M., Raven, E. & Hall, J. (2023). A multi-hazard risk framework to stress-test water supply systems to climate-related disruptions. *Earth's Future*, 11(1), article e2022EF002946. DOI 10.1029/2022EF002946 (January 2023) — [AGU/Wiley page](https://agupubs.onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/abs/10.1029/2022EF002946); [ResearchGate listing](https://www.researchgate.net/publication/366722630_A_multi-hazard_risk_framework_to_stress-test_water_supply_systems_to_climate-related_disruptions)
- **Oxford authors:** Olivia Becher did her DPhil in the Oxford OPSIS group (ECI), supervised by Jim Hall and Raghav Pant. Her thesis was "Stress testing large-scale water system resilience: a multi-risk framework for guiding adaptation". Hall and Pant are Oxford (OPSIS/ECI) staff, and Paltan has an Oxford Geography staff page. The printed byline affiliations were **not opened** — [OPSIS author page](https://opsis.eci.ox.ac.uk/author/olivia-becher); [Paltan, Oxford Geography](https://www.geog.ox.ac.uk/staff/hpaltan.html); [JBA–Oxford collaboration note](https://www.jbarisk.com/about-us/news/jba-risk-management-teams-up-with-oxford-university-for-infrastructure-study/)
- **The decision tool:** a general multi-hazard risk framework for water-supply systems, using customer disruption days as the common metric for comparing hazards, applied to the national network of Jamaica under present and future climate extremes — [AGU abstract](https://agupubs.onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/abs/10.1029/2022EF002946)
- **The metric (our formalisation of the standard risk integral):** for hazard h with event set {e} and annual probabilities p_e, expected annual CDD = Σ_h Σ_e p_e · Σ_customers (days without supply | e). The exact estimator in the paper was **not seen**.
- **Main results:** high-probability droughts produce more CDD than asset-damage events. Extreme asset-damage events (days) can cause more widespread CDD than droughts (months). Customers currently face an island-wide average of about 5 days of climate-related supply disruption per year. Disruptions roughly double by century's end even under a best-case emissions scenario and could rise by a factor of 2.5 under pessimistic scenarios — [AGU abstract](https://agupubs.onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/abs/10.1029/2022EF002946); [AGU press release via phys.org](https://phys.org/news/2023-02-climate-resilient.html); [PreventionWeb summary](https://www.preventionweb.net/news/how-build-climate-resilient-water-supply)

### Inferences
- **What it modifies:** the **objective** and the **scenario set**. MONTIMI-GO currently tests a single goal, the sea fraction or chloride at the screen against 250 mg/L, mainly under slow drivers. A CDD-type objective would integrate three channels: (i) drought, giving low f_R, high u and a slow rise of J; (ii) storm surge or sea-level extremes, a transient rise of h_s at the sea boundary; (iii) fast asset or power outages from floods. Each is expressed as days the well cannot deliver water within the standard, weighted by the customers served.
- **Direction of the decision:** the placement becomes the one that minimises E[CDD_sal] (with a CVaR guard) rather than the worst-case peak chloride. This can favour redundancy (two smaller screens far apart, or one inland reserve well) over one "safest" location. It also adds monitoring that fires fast during surges, alongside the annual chloride sample.
- **Innovation idea — "salinity disruption-days risk curve and placement rule":** define CDD_sal(x, b) = N_x · Σ_t 1{J_t(x,b) > 250} over a stress-test set {drought (AR(1), φ = 0.7) × surge return levels (from P1-type extremes) × private-abstraction scenarios}. Compute the exceedance curve P(CDD_sal > d). Test numerically whether the CDD-optimal (x, b) differs from today's max-chloride-optimal one, and by how many expected disruption days per year. A conjecture to test: under a monotone response of J to f_R and h_s, the two rules coincide only when one hazard channel dominates.

### Gaps
- The full-text equations, the per-hazard CDD split and the network-model details were not read. Numbers come from the abstract and press coverage.
- Becher's printed affiliation was not seen. The Oxford link rests on the OPSIS DPhil record and on Hall and Pant as co-authors.

## P5 — Jesson et al. (2022), sensitivity bounds for causal effects of a *continuous* intervention under hidden confounding (Themes 4 causal + 1 decision under uncertainty)

### Takeaway
The paper gives a continuous-treatment marginal sensitivity model (CMSM). For a user-chosen level Λ of hidden confounding it yields bounds on the dose–response curve that agree with the observed data, with scalable deep-ensemble estimators, and was developed with Oxford climate scientists for aerosol–cloud effects. This is the missing piece of our anchor-regression alarm: pumping is a continuous "dose", and unrecorded private abstraction during drought is a hidden confounder outside the anchor span.

### Cited Findings
- **Citation:** Jesson, A., Douglas, A., Manshausen, P., Solal, M., Meinshausen, N., Stier, P., Gal, Y. & Shalit, U. (2022). Scalable sensitivity and uncertainty analyses for causal-effect estimates of continuous-valued interventions. In *Advances in Neural Information Processing Systems 35 (NeurIPS 2022)*, main conference track (page range not verified; NeurIPS proceedings carry no DOI). arXiv:2204.10022 (v4, Oct 2022) — [NeurIPS proceedings abstract](https://proceedings.neurips.cc/paper_files/paper/2022/hash/5a29c3d172b80bab1238ddc227246c52-Abstract.html); [paper PDF](https://papers.neurips.cc/paper_files/paper/2022/file/5a29c3d172b80bab1238ddc227246c52-Paper-Conference.pdf); [arXiv](https://arxiv.org/abs/2204.10022v4); [code "overcast"](https://github.com/anndvision/overcast)
- **Oxford authors:** the paper is listed by OATML (Oxford Applied and Theoretical Machine Learning group, Department of Computer Science) and on the Oxford climate research site. Jesson and Gal are OATML; Manshausen, Douglas and Stier are Oxford atmospheric physics (AOPP). The printed byline was **not opened**. Note that some listings spell two names "Manschausen" and "Meinschausen"; NeurIPS/arXiv spellings are authoritative — [OATML page](https://oatml.cs.ox.ac.uk/publications/202204_Jesson_scalable_sensitivity.html); [climate.ox.ac.uk listing](https://www.climate.ox.ac.uk/node/3889191)
- **The tool:** the CMSM, which derives bounds "that agree with the observed data and a researcher-defined level of hidden confounding". The motivation is that ignorability (all confounders observed) and positivity (all treatment levels observed for every covariate value) are problematic for continuous treatments. A scalable algorithm and uncertainty-aware deep models estimate the bounds on large, high-dimensional observational data — [NeurIPS abstract](https://proceedings.neurips.cc/paper_files/paper/2022/hash/5a29c3d172b80bab1238ddc227246c52-Abstract.html)
- **Math, in essence:** for treatment t, covariates x and hidden confounder u, the model bounds how far the observable generalized propensity p(t | x) may differ from the complete propensity that also conditions on the hidden factor: Λ⁻¹ ≤ p(t | x) / p(t | x, u) ≤ Λ. For each Λ ≥ 1 it returns the interval [μ⁻(x,t;Λ), μ⁺(x,t;Λ)] for the dose–response μ(x,t) = E[Y_t | X = x]. Λ = 1 recovers the unconfounded estimate. The exact conditioning variable in the ratio (u versus the potential outcome y_t) and the bound formulas were **not checked against the PDF**.
- **Application and later work:** the application is the effect of human emissions (aerosols) on cloud properties using about 15 years of satellite observations — [NeurIPS abstract](https://proceedings.neurips.cc/paper_files/paper/2022/hash/5a29c3d172b80bab1238ddc227246c52-Abstract.html). A 2024 follow-up preprint uses it as baseline and reports sharper bounds at lower computation time. That claim comes from the follow-up's own abstract, not from independent testing — [arXiv 2411.02231](https://web3.arxiv.org/abs/2411.02231)

### Inferences
- **What it modifies:** the SPIRANCA-GO **margin and its validity domain**. Anchor regression certifies shifts that lie in the span of the recorded anchors (r, b̃). Private abstraction u during drought is unrecorded, rises with both drought and the utility's own pumping b, and raises chloride. It is a hidden confounder of b → J. The CMSM gives an interval for the interventional chloride curve E[J | do(b)] at confounding level Λ. A Λ-robust margin is m_Λ(b) = q_{1−α}(b) + δ̄·max(0, ℓ(b)) + [μ⁺(b;Λ) − μ̂(b)].
- **Positivity link:** the paper names positivity failure for continuous doses as a core problem. This is exactly our known failure mode: after a known pumping decision the screen moves into a regime the record never saw. The CMSM bounds widen where data are thin, so they flag that regime *before* the yearly chloride re-anchoring sample.
- **Direction of the decision:** pumping is more conservative where confounding by private abstraction cannot be ruled out (high-b, dry-year cells). The upper bound μ⁺ also identifies where a single extra chloride sample, or a survey of private wells, reduces the most uncertainty. That is a value-of-information target.
- **Innovation idea — the "Λ*-robustness value" of the alarm:** define Λ* = inf{Λ : μ⁺(b;Λ) + q_{1−α}(b) + δ̄·max(0, ℓ(b)) ≥ 250 mg/L} at the planned b. It is the smallest hidden-confounding strength that would overturn the "safe" verdict, and is reported to stakeholders like a tipping point. Numerical test: in MONTIMI-GO, generate u_t = c·(−r_t) + d·b_t + noise, fit the two-anchor alarm without u, then measure (i) the alarm miss rate versus c and d, (ii) whether Λ*-based margins repair it, and (iii) how many chloride samples per year the repair saves compared with re-anchoring alone.

### Gaps
- Exact CMSM equations and the bound-computation algorithm were not read from the PDF. The page range in the NeurIPS proceedings is not verified.
- NeurIPS is a refereed conference, not a journal. This matches "top refereed conference" in the brief.

## P6 (alternate) — Anderson et al. (2022), statistical attribution of land-cover change on streamflow: panel regression versus single-site models (Theme 4 causal/attribution)

### Takeaway
Across 729 US catchments (1992–2018), a panel (pooled, fixed-effects-type) regression finds a small but highly significant urbanisation effect on mean and high flows. Single-site models give widely varying coefficients whose median is not significant. Pooled "average causal effects" can therefore hide site-level heterogeneity. For us, a pumping→chloride effect estimated across wells may not hold at our particular screen.

### Cited Findings
- **Citation:** Anderson, B. J., Slater, L. J., Dadson, S. J., Blum, A. G. & Prosdocimi, I. (2022). Statistical attribution of the influence of urban and tree cover change on streamflow: a comparison of large sample statistical approaches. *Water Resources Research*, 58(5), article e2021WR030742. DOI 10.1029/2021WR030742 — [AGU/Wiley page](https://agupubs.onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/full/10.1029/2021WR030742); [NORA copy](https://nora.nerc.ac.uk/id/eprint/532783/). **Author-list conflict:** one search summary listed only Anderson, Prosdocimi, Slater and Dadson. Another, citing the ORA/NORA records, gives the five authors above. Check against the publisher page before citing.
- **Oxford authors:** Bailey J. Anderson (Oxford DPhil, completed 2024, supervised by Simon Dadson and Louise Slater), with Slater and Dadson of Oxford Geography. The paper is held in ORA. The printed byline was **not opened** — [ORA record](https://ora.ox.ac.uk/objects/uuid:25563f36-a1e3-477a-96a4-5b8f64b4ace0); [Anderson's Oxford graduate page](https://www.geog.ox.ac.uk/graduate/research/banderson.html)
- **Main quantitative results:** in the panel regression, each 1-percentage-point increase in urban area is associated with a small but highly significant increase in mean and high flows of about 0.6–0.7%. Tree-cover change has no strongly significant effect. The median single-site GLM coefficients show no significant relationship between either land-cover change and any streamflow quantile, with large variation across sites. The authors conclude that large-sample methods are useful for attribution, but substantial variability remains — [AGU/Wiley page](https://agupubs.onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/full/10.1029/2021WR030742)
- **Math (generic form, not copied from the paper):** log Q^{(q)}_{it} = α_i + β·Urban_{it} + θ·Tree_{it} + γᵀ(climate controls)_{it} + ε_{it}, with site effects α_i, compared against site-by-site GLMs Q^{(q)}_{it} ~ β_i·Urban_{it} + …. Whether the paper also uses year effects or other controls was **not verified**. An earlier EGU21 abstract used 2,865 catchments and three approaches, including GAMLSS. The journal version is the one to cite — [EGU21-2282](https://meetingorganizer.copernicus.org/EGU21/EGU21-2282.html)

### Inferences
- **What it modifies:** how the pumping→chloride slope is estimated and transferred to the decision screen. With several monitored wells, a pooled estimate β̂ of ∂Cl/∂b is tempting. The paper's result says the pooled β̂ can be "significant on average" while the site-specific β_i at our screen differs a lot, for example where clay lenses (our two immobile zones) delay or damp the response.
- **Direction of the decision:** the margin should cover site heterogeneity, i.e., use β_i at the screen rather than the pooled β̄. This makes pumping more conservative at screens with poorly identified β_i. It also adds monitoring at the screens with the widest posterior on β_i.
- **Innovation idea — "panel-heterogeneity margin":** fit a random-slope panel, Cl_{it} = α_i + (β̄ + v_i)·b̃_{it} + λ·r_{it} + ε_{it}, with v_i ~ (0, τ²). Inflate the SPIRANCA-GO margin by z_{1−α}·τ·|Δb| for a planned change Δb. Test on synthetic multi-well MONTIMI-GO transects with heterogeneous silt/clay memory, and check whether the pooled-slope alarm under-covers at the slow-memory screen.

### Gaps
- The full model specification and the sizes of the confidence intervals were not read.
- The author list needs a final check because of the conflicting search summaries.

## P7 (backup, weaker affiliation check) — Salehabadi et al. (2022), plausible severe multi-year droughts for the Colorado River (Theme 3 drought; supports Theme 1)

### Takeaway
The paper builds three drought scenarios, each with 100 streamflow sequences, from historical and tree-ring-reconstructed flows at Lees Ferry for use in system-operation models. It finds that the drought that began in 2000 has an average flow far below the historical record. It is useful for stress-testing multi-year drought persistence beyond our AR(1) φ = 0.7. However, the Oxford affiliation of co-author Kevin Wheeler was not seen on the byline.

### Cited Findings
- **Citation:** Salehabadi, H., Tarboton, D. G., Udall, B., Wheeler, K. G. & Schmidt, J. C. (2022). An assessment of potential severe droughts in the Colorado River Basin. *JAWRA Journal of the American Water Resources Association*, 58(6), 1053–1075. DOI 10.1111/1752-1688.13061 — [Wiley page](https://onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/10.1111/1752-1688.13061)
- **Results and data:** natural flow at Lees Ferry is the basin's main runoff metric. Three drought scenarios with 100 streamflow sequences each were produced for system operations models. The post-2000 drought has an average flow far below the historical record. The authors conclude that Colorado River management needs rethinking to prepare for future droughts — [Wiley page](https://onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/10.1111/1752-1688.13061). The data and scripts, including a duration–severity and cumulative-deficit analysis, are archived — [HydroShare scenarios](https://www.hydroshare.org/resource/15693b79fc6b47ccbe6dd42c7b734d98/); [HydroShare duration–severity analysis](https://hydroshare.org/resource/bbe8dffacb07458783b2e6924aa615bb/)
- **Oxford affiliation (unverified on this paper):** Wheeler is described as a Senior Research Fellow at Oxford's Environmental Change Institute in a 2023 seminar listing. In 2022 he led the Oxford-listed Science Policy Forum "What will it take to stabilize the Colorado River?" (Science 377, 373–375; its DOI could **not** be confirmed by search) — [Politecnico di Milano seminar listing](https://www.deib.polimi.it/eng/events/details/2790); [OPSIS publication page](https://opsis.eci.ox.ac.uk/publication/wheeler-4/); [Oxford Martin School news](https://www.oxfordmartin.ox.ac.uk/news/current-policies-cannot-stabilise-the-colorado-river)
- **Decision rule from the Science piece:** trigger Lower Basin and Mexico cuts on the *combined* storage of Lakes Powell and Mead rather than Mead alone. Combined storage fell from about 95% full in 2000 to a projected ~25% by end-2022 — [Oxford Martin School news](https://www.oxfordmartin.ox.ac.uk/news/current-policies-cannot-stabilise-the-colorado-river)

### Inferences
- **What it modifies:** the drought-persistence input. Our AR(1) with φ = 0.7 has geometrically decaying run lengths. Tree-ring-informed scenarios with multi-decadal deficits test whether the 250 mg/L goal survives droughts longer than AR(1) predicts. Cumulative deficit D_d = Σ_{k<d}(r̄ − r_{t−k}), not the annual anomaly, is the driver of slow salinisation with clay memory.
- **Innovation idea:** a "cumulative-storage trigger" for pumping, the analogue of the combined-storage rule. Cut b when a model-based index of total freshwater storage in the section (fresh lens plus the immobile-zone inventory) crosses a threshold, rather than when the screen chloride does. Test with MONTIMI-GO under AR(1) versus long-memory drought sequences.

### Gaps
- Wheeler's affiliation on the JAWRA byline and the Science DOI are both unverified. Use this paper only if one of P1–P6 is rejected.
