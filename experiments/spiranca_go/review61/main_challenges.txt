1. Extremes beyond the record. Record-shattering droughts and intense rainfall push the aquifer into states the monitoring record never contained; in our tests, an alarm built on the record alone failed after the pumping decision in 9 of 20 aquifers.
2. Hidden human feedback. Unregistered private wells pump more in a drought. This changes the causal mechanism, is not seen in the recorded rainfall and pumping data, and must therefore be bounded rather than estimated.
3. Coupled, slow physics at low cost. Density-driven flow, salt memory in clay and silt layers and ion exchange act over decades, yet every decision must be checked over many storylines and weather sequences; this needs a coupled Newton solver, an exact adjoint and goal-oriented simplification.
4. Sparse data. Chloride is sampled at few wells and rarely, piezometers are few, and sea level and recharge are uncertain; deciding where and when to sample is part of the problem.
5. From mathematics to decisions. Stakeholders must agree on the acceptable share of seasons above 250 mg/L, on who gives up water, the utility or the farmers, and on the rules that trigger pumping cuts. The trade-off is sharp: the safest tested rule delivers 71% of the planned water in the worst storyline.
6. Validation and transfer. Results come from synthetic aquifers on a 2-D section; they must be confirmed with Fushë-Kuqe field data, extended to 3-D and transferred to other coastal and karst aquifers.
