Seawater intrusion is well understood physically: variable-density codes such as SEAWAT simulate it (Langevin et al., 2008), and reviews summarise its processes and management (Werner et al., 2013). Pumping is now optimised under parameter and climate uncertainty with ensembles and sharp-interface models (Coulon et al., 2022, 2024; Mostafaei-Avandari & Ketabchi, 2020), and monitoring networks are designed for adaptive management (Sreekanth & Datta, 2014). For decisions under deep uncertainty, robust decision making and adaptive pathways stress-test strategies against many futures (Lempert et al., 2006; Haasnoot et al., 2013). Recent work extends the futures beyond the observed record with pooled forecast ensembles (Kelder et al., 2020) and machine-learning corrections for rare extremes (Barthel Sorensen et al., 2024), turns data directly into decisions (Bertsimas & Kallus, 2020), and gives guarantees under distribution shift through anchor regression (Rothenhäusler et al., 2021) or exact conformal tests for counterfactuals (Chernozhukov et al., 2021).

Three gaps remain. First, coastal management models rarely couple the full density-dependent physics with an explicit, stakeholder-facing decision rule solved in the same time loop. Second, statistical early warnings of salinisation are seldom certified, and their validity after a pumping decision or under record-shattering drought is rarely tested. Third, unrecorded behaviour, such as private abstraction that rises in droughts, and the gap between rainfall and effective recharge are treated as noise rather than as bounded causal shifts. This project addresses all three.
